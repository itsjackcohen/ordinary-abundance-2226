A Companion Edition · The Year 2226

The awe we let go ordinary

An evening in a home two centuries from now — where the miracles are the ones we made.

The original Ordinary Abundance borrows dead voices to restore our wonder at recorded music, electric light, clean water. This edition runs the trick forward: it imagines a curator in 2226 walking through an ordinary night, pausing at objects that bore them with familiarity — and quoting us, the people of 1990–2100, marvelling at what we had just begun to build. The quotes here are imagined; the longing in them is not.

Eight o'clock

The thinking room

The room is quiet, but you are not alone in it. You ask the air a half-formed question and it answers in your grandmother's cadence — she has been gone sixty years. A wall softens into a window onto a shoreline that does not exist. You settle in to think, and the room thinks alongside you.

A mind to talk to
It listened, it reasoned, it wrote back — and I kept forgetting there was no one there. We had spent the whole of our history alone inside our own heads. Now the head had company.
A working writer, 2024 imagined

For all of prior history, thinking was a solitary act; counsel meant finding another human who had the time. The first machines that could hold a real conversation arrived in the 2020s and were, briefly, astonishing — before they became the quiet furniture of every room.

The voices of the dead, restored
My father died before my daughter could speak. Tonight she asked him a question and he laughed the way he used to, and answered her. I do not know what to call this. I only know I wept.
Unattributed, c. 2050 imagined

Photographs froze a likeness; recordings froze a voice. But to reconstruct a person well enough to converse — that waited until the mid-century. Early users described it as grief interrupted, a door they had thought permanently shut left ajar.

Any place, on any wall
The window shows Lisbon in the rain, then the surface of a comet, then the room my mother grew up in. We used to travel a week to see a coastline. Now the coastline comes to the wall, and waits.
Design notes for an early spatial display, 2038 imagined

André Malraux dreamed of a "museum without walls." His heirs built the wall that could become any place at all — the ordinary background of a 2226 evening, glanced at as absently as we glance at a lamp.

Nine o'clock

The kitchen

You are not hungry so much as curious. You name a dish your great-grandmother used to make and could never quite write down; the counter builds it from the memory of her hands. The meat was never an animal. The fruit never crossed an ocean, because the tree is three rooms away.

Meat without the animal
It cooked like flesh, browned like flesh, tasted like the thing itself — and nothing had died for it. My grandchildren will find it strange that this was ever strange.
A food scientist, 2032 imagined

For all of history, meat meant a killed creature and the vast machinery of raising it. Cultivated protein arrived quietly in the 2030s and within a generation made the old way look not evil so much as unimaginable — the way we now regard chaining a book to a desk.

Fruit that never travelled
Charles Lamb rented a pineapple for the evening rather than eat it. I grow three kinds in the pantry wall, and forget to pick them. I am ashamed of my forgetting and grateful for its cause.
A home journal, 2071 imagined

A pineapple was once a costly spectacle; a strawberry in winter, a small miracle of ice and rail and ship. When the growing came home — when the ocean and the season stopped mattering — abundance stopped being an occasion and became a backdrop.

The recipe of a hand now gone
She never measured anything. We lost the bread when we lost her. Now the kitchen has her touch by heart, and the loaf comes out warm and slightly wrong in exactly the way she made it wrong. That wrongness is the whole inheritance.
Unattributed, c. 2060 imagined

Skill used to die with the person. The tacit knowledge in a pair of hands — the pinch, the feel, the not-quite-a-recipe — was the most perishable thing we owned. Capturing it, imperfections and all, turned family cooking from memory into presence.

Ten o'clock

The quiet room

The night winds down. You take a drop that keeps the years at bay, the way your ancestors took a nightly pill for a heart they could not otherwise trust. A scan you did not notice reads you head to foot and finds nothing worth waking you for. You sleep because the body, at last, is a solved thing.

Cancer, made a nuisance
They caught it at a size no eye could see, corrected it in an afternoon, and sent me home. My mother's generation heard the word and heard a sentence. I heard the word and heard an appointment.
A patient's note, 2044 imagined

This is the direct heir to smallpox made historical. What Jefferson wrote of Jenner's vaccine — that future nations would know the affliction "only by history" — came true again, one dreaded disease at a time, until the dread itself thinned to routine.

The years, slowed
My knees at ninety are the knees I had at fifty. The great surprise was not living longer — it was that the extra decades arrived unbroken, that age stopped being a slow subtraction.
A physician, 2068 imagined

Morton's epitaph read: "since whom, science has controlled pain." The mid-century sequel controlled decline. Not death — decline: the fraying of the body that every prior human took for the price of years. To 2226 a frail old age is a historical curiosity, like the frozen washbasin.

A doctor that never sleeps
It watched the rhythm of my heart for a year and knew, before I did, the morning something changed. Care used to mean noticing you were already sick. Now it means being noticed before you are.
Health diary, 2035 imagined

Medicine was long a thing you sought out once you already suffered. Continuous, unobtrusive sensing turned it inside out — from rescue to vigilance — so completely that the people of 2226 cannot imagine a body left unwatched.

Half past ten

The travelling room

You have a meeting tomorrow on another continent and it will cost you an hour, not a day. Your closest friend lives where the air is thin and red; you will see them at breakfast. You lie down and, half-asleep, wonder idly what people did back when a life was lived within a week's travel of where it began.

The ocean, made an hour
Wilbur Wright said flight was an ideal handed down by ancestors who watched the birds. We kept the ideal but lost the awe — I board, I read a chapter, I land on the far side of the earth, mildly annoyed at the queue.
A frequent traveller, 2058 imagined

Emigration once meant never seeing your family again. Then it meant a week at sea, then a day in the air, then an hour. Each generation inherited the previous one's miracle as its baseline of boredom.

A face across the void
We spoke every night — her on the red plains, me in the old house — and I could see the dust on her collar. We had made the telegraph a perpetual miracle. This was the miracle that let me watch my sister live on another world.
Letters home, c. 2090 imagined

Briggs and Maverick called the telegraph "a perpetual miracle, which no familiarity can render commonplace." They were wrong about the familiarity — every marvel of contact eventually goes commonplace — but right that the reaching never stops. By 2226 the void it crosses is measured in planets.

Sleep, finally understood
The bed reads the day out of me and gives back exactly the rest I'm owed. That last human on the old internet wondered what people slept on before mattresses. I lie here and wonder what people slept on before sleep itself was understood.
Bedside note, 2077 imagined

The original site ends on a small, human thought — what did people sleep on before mattresses? This edition answers it and asks the next question in the same chain. That restlessness, the reaching past the newest comfort toward the next, is the one thing every century in this house has shared.

Eleven o'clock

The parlor

The house grows quieter, but the talk in the parlor is not finished. This is where the family lingers over the miracles too diffuse to live in any single room — the weather outside, the language between strangers, the walls that remember, the goodbyes that were finally allowed to end. It is the room where the house thinks out loud about everything the other rooms forgot to mention.

Weather, made an appointment
We asked for snow the morning she was born, just once, just for the window. It fell exactly on time, and stopped exactly on time, and I have never gotten over asking for something so large and receiving it.
A new parent, 2091 imagined

Cloud seeding began in 1946, a clumsy flare fired into a reluctant cloud in the hope of coaxing out rain. It took a century and a half to go from nudging weather to scheduling it — but the ambition, and the audacity, were there from the very first flare.

A house that remembers where you left off
I came back to the reading chair after three years away and the light was already the light I'd left it in, the page still open where I'd stopped mid-sentence. The house had been waiting instead of moving on.
A returning traveller, 2065 imagined

The "smart home" of the early 2000s could unlock a door or dim a light; it could not hold a place for you. What 2226 no longer remarks on is the difference between a house that reacts to a person and one that has quietly kept their spot.

A stranger, fully understood
She spoke only the old mountain dialect, the one with maybe four hundred living speakers left, and I understood every joke she made — including the ones that only work in that language. Neither of us noticed the machine between us until she pointed it out, laughing, an hour later.
A journal from a border town, 2077 imagined

The Tower of Babel is the oldest story we have about the ache of not understanding a stranger. Machine translation arrived, clumsily, in the 2010s, mangling idiom and losing every joke. History does not record the exact year the mangling quietly stopped.

A goodbye that was allowed to finish
We kept my mother close for a year — her laugh in the kitchen, her voice on the stairs — and then, as she'd asked us to before she died, we let the house grow quiet again. I was afraid the silence would feel like losing her twice. It felt like keeping my promise.
A family record, 2098 imagined

The thinking room of this house marvelled at a voice restored; this room admits what that first excitement hadn't yet learned — that a presence with no ending can become its own quiet grief. The wisest households eventually built the goodbye in on purpose.

A street with no engines in it
My grandfather said the old avenues used to roar all day, every day — a sound so constant that people stopped hearing it as sound at all. I stood on that same avenue last week and heard rain landing on leaves two blocks away.
A city planner's notebook, 2071 imagined

New York convened its first Noise Abatement Commission in 1930 and named honking the city's second most serious health hazard. It took nearly two centuries to get from a committee report to a street quiet enough to hear the weather change.

A house grown, not built
We didn't buy the house, or build it. We planted it, on a Tuesday, and by the following spring it had grown itself a kitchen. I still don't know how to explain the pride I feel over a home I did not construct with my own hands.
A home-grower's notebook, 2085 imagined

This is the fruit that never travelled trick, played on shelter instead of food. Once a tree could be brought into the pantry, it was only a matter of time before a house could be grown instead of assembled — and the deed, the mortgage, the down payment, became as quaint as a rented pineapple.

A room the first edition could not build

What we had, and they recovered

Every prior room borrowed our era's awe at the things we gained. This one is different. Here the curator of 2226 keeps the objects they had to win back — the ordinary goods we held and lost in our own century, and the quotes come not from wonder but from mourning. In this room, the astonished voices are ours.

A stable sky
My grandmother described seasons as if they were reliable — a winter you could name, a coast that stayed where the maps said. I have only read about weather you could trust.
A child of the 2040s, remembering imagined

The generations of 1990–2100 inherited a climate their ancestors never thought to marvel at, because it had always simply been there. They were the first to watch it become an occasion — and the long work of steadying it again is the pride of the century that followed.

Air you did not have to check
There was a summer we measured the sky each morning before letting the children out. My great-grandparents would not have understood the sentence. Clean air had been, for them, a thing you simply breathed.
Unattributed, 2055 imagined

Croton water was once cause for a city to weep with joy. Air of that same taken-for-granted purity slipped, for a while, out of the ordinary — and its return is one of the quiet victories the people of 2226 no longer notice.

The night sky, unlit
They lit Wabash, Indiana, and stood in awe of a square as bright as noon. We lit the whole earth, and lost the stars to do it. My grandchildren saw the Milky Way for the first time last year and did not have a word for it.
A stargazer, 2061 imagined

Electric light was the marvel that opened the original house. Its shadow was a sky washed out by our own glow. That the darkness — and the stars in it — could be given back is a wonder no one in 1880 would have thought to wish for.

Silence, and unhurried attention
My mind was never quiet. Something always pulled at it. I read once that people used to sit in a chair with a single book and a single lamp and simply be there, for an hour, uninterrupted. I have spent my life trying to learn that trick.
A journal from the 2030s imagined

The original site opens with a person picking up a book and settling into a chair to read. That undivided quiet — the plainest luxury in the first house — became, in our century, one of the hardest to keep. Its recovery required no invention, only the decision to protect it.

Ordinary Abundance, 2226

Edward Bellamy imagined that music on demand would be "the limit of human felicity." He set the bar exactly one invention too low, as everyone always does. The people of 2226 walk past cured cancers and resurrected voices and worlds a breakfast away, and feel nothing in particular — the same nothing we feel walking past a tap of clean water.

The lesson of this house is not that the objects change. It is that the awe is always leaking out of the present and pooling in the past, where a good curator can go and fetch it back. Every generation lives inside a gallery of the previous one's heroics, and calls it Tuesday.

It is to humanity's credit that we remain restless in the midst of all of this progress.

That one line, lifted whole from the first edition, is the only sentence in this house that needs no updating. The miracles turn over. The restlessness does not. It is what built every room here, and it is already at work on the rooms neither we nor the people of 2226 can yet picture.
